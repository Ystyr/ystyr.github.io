About

I'm Aleksei (Lex) Andreev. I started in game development — C++, large codebases, real-time simulation — and kept following the interesting problems outward: rendering pipelines, procedural systems, tools that let other people build, firmware that talks to physical machines, CAD geometry that has to survive contact with a factory floor.

What ties it together isn't a single industry. It's a way of working: understand the actual constraint, prototype fast, measure, then make it reliable enough that someone else can depend on it. I've done that for location-based AR, a shipped Meta Quest arcade title, industrial robots, and the Unity developers who download my tools from the Asset Store.

These days I'm most energized by performance-critical systems — computational geometry, GPU work, simulation, architecture — wherever the gap between "it works in a demo" and "it works every day" is where the real engineering lives.

Work

Factory-floor geometry

Augmentus Robotics · Senior Software Developer · 2025–2026

Most recently I worked on software that sits between a CAD file and a robot arm — the unglamorous middle where a STEP model has to be cleaned, filtered, understood, and turned into something a path planner can actually use. Off-the-shelf topology tools weren't fast enough for the datasets we were seeing, so I built a custom analysis system from scratch: feature extraction, geometric reasoning, the kind of code where a wrong assumption wastes an afternoon and a right one saves a production line hours.

That work sat inside a broader OpenCascade pipeline — import, repair, select, prepare — alongside integrity protection for deployed builds and the ordinary maintenance that keeps a platform alive while new features land.

When VR meets real hardware

LAI Games · Senior Game Programmer · 2021–2024

At LAI Games the job wasn't just "make it fun in the headset." It was make the headset believe the cabinet is real. Over three years I worked across VR gameplay and systems in Unity, firmware for custom arcade cabinet hardware, and middleware that kept physical devices and headset software in sync — so a button press on a real cabinet arrives as the right event in VR. I helped design the virtual arcade machine systems that hold the illusion together: the part where VR stops feeling like a menu and starts feeling like a room full of working cabinets.

One of the most visible outcomes of that broader work is Arcade Legend — a shipped Meta Quest title where you restore an abandoned arcade, fill it with licensed games, and play with friends. It's on the Quest store today, not a prototype.

A city as a game board

KWEST · Lead Software Engineer · 2016–2021

My longest single project was Kwest Go — a location-based AR storytelling game built with Google, where the world outside your phone is part of the level design. I was lead engineer across years of production: Android and Unity systems, geolocation, narrative tooling, and the slow migration from one AR stack to another when the platform shifted under us.

"The game would not be what it is without Lex's work — if it would exist at all." — Omeed Shams, CEO of Kwest

Two pieces of that work still feel representative. Kwest Graph was our authoring system — a visual framework that let designers build interactive experiences without waiting on engineering, while still being deep enough to grow with the product. And when Google's MUSK map API drowned us in meshes and draw calls, I replaced the rendering path with something that could handle the scale — I wrote up how, including what I'd do differently now.

Earlier chapters

I cut my teeth at Suricate Games in St Petersburg — C++, the Unigine engine, large commercial projects, the ordinary grind of features and bug fixes that teaches you how real codebases actually behave. That was before I moved to Singapore.

Once here, I tried starting my own thing under Supervixi Automata — early prototypes, GPU-accelerated services, experiments in procedural content and distributed systems. It didn't become a company, but it was my first honest attempt at building something mine, and it set the pattern for everything that followed: find a hard idea, build the pipeline, see how far it goes.

Side projects

Tools for other builders

Game Soft Craft · Unity Asset Store

I publish under Game Soft Craft on the Unity Asset Store — small, focused tools for developers who'd rather spend time on their game than reinventing a wheel. The thread is procedural systems and sky rendering: S.P.A.C.E – Procedural Sky for atmosphere without hand-painted cubemaps, and OmniCA, a free cellular-automata tool for emergent patterns without writing the engine glue first. Same instinct as Kwest Graph, aimed at a different audience.

A sensor pod on the desk

Nordic Thingy:91 X · personal

Another tangent from the day job: the Thingy:91 X, Nordic's cellular IoT prototyping kit — sensors, BLE, LTE-M/NB-IoT, the whole low-power connected-device stack. I've been working through it with the nRF Connect SDK and Zephyr RTOS: firmware, GATT services, getting data off the device and onto a network without pretending a breadboard is a product. It's the same itch as the LAI Games firmware work, but on hardware I picked myself, at my own pace.

Teaching a body to move

AI Warehouse · volunteer

I've been helping on a YouTube ML project — building the training pipeline for a physically simulated AI character. Unity ML-Agents on the simulation side: environments, rewards, the loop that turns physics into learnable behaviour. I built the pipeline and ran the training myself. It's a different flavour of engineering from robotics CAD — less geometry, more iteration — but the same satisfaction when something clumsy slowly learns to look alive.
